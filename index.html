Learning to sing on your own

Short version: sing in your key, practise little and often, always compare what you sang with the target, and slowly take away the visual aids. The rest of this page explains why, then gives fixes for the problems beginners run into most.

What the research says

"Off-key" is rarely a hearing problem

Most people who sing out of tune can hear pitch differences fine. The problem is translating what they hear into the right vocal-fold settings: an ear→voice mapping problem. True tone-deafness (congenital amusia) is rare. Mapping problems respond to practice with feedback.

Pfordresher & Brown (2007), Music Perception 25(2); Hutchins & Peretz (2012), J. Exp. Psychol.: General 141(1).

Real-time visual feedback helps

Seeing your pitch as a line against the target improves pitch accuracy in training studies. It works best with a simple display that shows the whole contour, not a score alone.

Welch, Howard & Rush (1989), Psychology of Music 17(2); Wilson, Lee, Callaghan & Thorpe (2008), J. Interdisciplinary Music Studies.

…but don't lean on it forever

In motor learning, constant feedback during practice can boost performance while hurting retention, because you come to depend on it (the "guidance hypothesis"). So alternate: learn with the line, then sing with the song muted or in Blind mode and check afterwards.

Salmoni, Schmidt & Walter (1984), Psychological Bulletin 95(3).

Imitation is easier in your own range

Poor-pitch singers imitate recordings of their own voice more accurately than other voices. Matching a very different voice (e.g. a high male playback singer) is extra hard. Transpose to your key, and listen back to your own takes.

Pfordresher & Mantell (2014), Cognitive Psychology 70.

Warm up with semi-occluded exercises

Lip trills, humming and straw phonation create back-pressure that makes the vocal folds vibrate with less effort and impact. That makes them a safe, effective warm-up and good for working through register breaks.

Titze (2006), J. Speech, Language & Hearing Research 49(2).

Little and often, slow first

Distributed practice (20 minutes a day) consolidates motor skills better than one long weekly session. Practising fast ornaments slowly and accurately, then gradually speeding up, is standard both in motor-learning research and in the guru–shishya tradition (riyaz).

Spacing effect: e.g. Shea, Lai, Black & Park (2000), Human Movement Science 19.

Where the app can't help: a teacher can see and hear tension, posture and tone quality that a pitch tracker can't measure. If you can, get an occasional lesson (even online) to check technique, and use this app for daily riyaz between lessons.

How to learn a song like Unnai Kaanadhu Naan

  1. Build the melody guide in Song Studio. Check the detected Sa (phrases should come to rest on the Sa line), and see whether the melody fits your range.
  2. Pick your key. If lines go above your comfortable top, transpose the practice copy down, or sing an octave lower. Don't force high notes.
  3. Learn line by line. Click a line to loop it. First listen 3–4 times at 0.75× with the guide tone on, and watch the shape.
  4. Sing on "aa" first, words later. Get the melody and ornaments on a vowel, then add lyrics.
  5. Sargam it. Each line shows an approximate sargam. For a hard passage, type it into the Swara Pad and drill it slowly with the tanpura.
  6. Speed up in steps: 0.6× → 0.75× → 0.85× → 1×. Move on when you get two good takes in a row.
  7. Remove the crutches: switch to Song muted (guide tone only), then Blind mode, then sing with the original and listen back to your recording.

Fixes for common problems

I can't reach the high notes
  • First check the key. Transposing 2–3 semitones down is not cheating; playback singers' keys are chosen for their voices.
  • Don't push volume to go higher. Lighter is easier up there. Practise the high line on a lip trill or "ng" hum, then open to a vowel.
  • Narrow vowels help on high notes: "aa" → slightly towards "uh"/"aw". Keep the jaw loose and the chin level, not lifted.
  • Build range gradually: sirens and the 5-note scale moving up, a few semitones beyond comfortable, never to strain.
My voice cracks or breaks between low and high
  • The break is the transition between registers (chest-dominant and head-dominant). Everyone has it.
  • Sirens on a lip trill through the break, quietly, daily. The goal is a smooth blend ("mix"), not a louder chest voice.
  • Lower the volume as you approach the break, and don't let the throat squeeze.
I'm consistently flat (or sharp)
  • Flat: often low energy, tiredness, or a dark, swallowed vowel. Brighten the vowel slightly, give more breath support, keep the tanpura audible.
  • Sharp: usually tension or over-blowing. Sing softer, relax shoulders and jaw.
  • Check your microtone map: if one swara is off (e.g. Ga is sharp), drill it specifically with the pitch-match game.
My held notes wobble
  • Steadiness comes from steady airflow. Do the breath hiss and Sa sadhana daily.
  • Breathe low (belly and lower ribs), not high in the chest. Don't let the breath collapse at the end of the phrase.
"Two notes at once", the fast curly bits

What sounds like two notes at once is usually one of these ornaments, performed very fast:

  • Kan swar: a split-second touch of a neighbouring note before the main note. On the pitch line it's a little hook.
  • Murki: a quick 3–4 note turn (e.g. P–d–P–m), like a fast flourish.
  • Gamak / gamaka: a controlled oscillation between notes. In Carnatic music, gamakas define the raga.
  • Meend: a smooth glide through all the microtones between two notes.
  • Andolan: a slow, gentle swing on a note (e.g. komal Ga in some ragas).
  • Khatka: a sharp, emphasised cluster of notes, like a strong murki.

Method: loop the line at 0.5–0.6×, turn on the guide tone, look at the shape of the curve, and copy it on "aa". Then drill the ornament on its own (kan, gamak, meend). Speed comes last. A fast ornament sung loosely sounds worse than a slow one sung exactly.

What are microtones and do I need them?

Indian music places some notes slightly differently from a keyboard. Sung against a tanpura, shuddha Ga naturally sits around 386¢ above Sa (keyboard: 400¢), and certain ragas use characteristically low or high versions of a swara. Switch Swara positions to "Just intonation" to see these targets, and use the ±15¢ tolerance for fine work. For film songs, follow the singer's curve. The reference line already includes their microtones.

I run out of breath in long phrases
  • Plan breath points: listen for where the singer breathes, and mark them.
  • Inhale low and silently; don't start a long phrase with a loud, pushed onset.
  • Daily hiss exercise: aim for 25–30 s of even hiss.
My throat hurts

Stop for the day. Singing should never hurt. Common causes: singing too high or loud for too long, not warming up, dehydration, or pushing through a cold. Hoarseness or pain lasting more than two weeks, or a sudden loss of voice, needs an ENT doctor.

About this app

Everything runs in your browser: pitch detection (YIN), melody extraction from full song mixes (harmonic-summation salience with Viterbi tracking, in the spirit of Salamon & Gómez's Melodia), tanpura synthesis, and storage. Nothing you sing or record is uploaded. Melody extraction from a mix with instruments isn't perfect: if a flute or violin line gets picked up, use "Fix the melody guide" in Song Studio. Chrome or Edge on desktop recommended (needed for tab-audio capture).
