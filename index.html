Learn

How to learn to sing on your own. The short version: sing in your key, practise a little every day, always compare what you sang with the target, and slowly take away the visual aids. This works for any song, whether it's Jeff Buckley, a Tamil film song or a musical theatre number. Below: why, plus fixes for the most common problems.

What the research says

"Off-key" is rarely a hearing problem

Most people who sing out of tune can hear pitch differences fine. The problem is translating what they hear into the right vocal-fold settings: an ear→voice mapping problem. True tone-deafness (congenital amusia) is rare. Mapping problems respond to practice with feedback.

Pfordresher & Brown (2007), Music Perception 25(2); Hutchins & Peretz (2012), J. Exp. Psychol.: General 141(1).

Real-time visual feedback helps

Seeing your pitch as a line against the target improves pitch accuracy in training studies. It works best with a simple display that shows the whole contour, not a score alone.

Welch, Howard & Rush (1989), Psychology of Music 17(2); Wilson, Lee, Callaghan & Thorpe (2008), J. Interdisciplinary Music Studies.

…but don't lean on it forever

In motor learning, constant feedback during practice can boost performance while hurting retention, because you come to depend on it (the "guidance hypothesis"). So alternate: learn with the line, then sing from memory (Guide only, or Test yourself) and check afterwards. The line coach fades the help out step by step for exactly this reason.

Salmoni, Schmidt & Walter (1984), Psychological Bulletin 95(3).

Imitation is easier in your own range

Poor-pitch singers imitate recordings of their own voice more accurately than other voices. Copying a very different voice (a high tenor, a female singer, a playback singer) is extra hard. Transpose to your key, and listen back to your own takes.

Pfordresher & Mantell (2014), Cognitive Psychology 70.

Warm up with semi-occluded exercises

Lip trills, humming and straw phonation create back-pressure that lets the vocal folds vibrate with less effort and impact. That makes them a safe, effective warm-up, and good for smoothing out register breaks.

Titze (2006), J. Speech, Language & Hearing Research 49(2).

Little and often, slow first

Distributed practice (20 minutes a day) consolidates motor skills better than one long weekly session. Practising fast passages slowly and accurately, then gradually speeding up, is standard in motor-learning research and in every singing tradition.

Spacing effect: e.g. Shea, Lai, Black & Park (2000), Human Movement Science 19.

Where the app can't help: a teacher can see and hear tension, posture and tone quality that a pitch tracker can't measure. If you can, get an occasional lesson (even online) to check technique, and use this app for daily practice in between.

How to learn any song

  1. Build the melody guide on the Songs page. Check the Key chip (phrases usually come to rest on the bold line) and the Range chip: a green dot means it fits your voice.
  2. Pick your key. If lines go above your comfortable top, transpose down or sing an octave lower. Original keys suit the original singer, not you.
  3. Learn line by line. Press Learn on a line (or on any stretch you looped). The coach takes you through it: listen, sing slowly with the guide, sing with the singer, then from memory. It points at the exact notes that went off and lets you drill just the hard part.
  4. Sing on "ah" first, words later. Get the melody and ornaments on a vowel, then add lyrics.
  5. Read the notes. Each line shows its words and approximate notes. For a passage that needs extra work, type it into the Note Pad and drill it slowly over the drone.
  6. Speed up in steps: 0.6× → 0.75× → 0.85× → 1×. Move on when you get two good takes in a row.
  7. Remove the crutches: sing with Guide only (song muted, guide tone on), then Test yourself (your line shows only afterwards), then with the original, and listen back to your recording.

Fixes for common problems

I can't reach the high notes
  • First check the key. Transposing 2–3 semitones down is not cheating; singers pick keys for their own voices.
  • Don't push volume to go higher. Lighter is easier up there. Practise the high line on a lip trill or "ng" hum, then open to a vowel.
  • Narrow the vowel on high notes ("ah" towards "uh"). Keep the jaw loose and the chin level, not lifted.
  • Build range gradually: sirens and the 5-note scale moving up, a little beyond comfortable, never to strain.
My voice cracks or flips between low and high
  • The break is the transition between chest voice and head voice (or falsetto). Everyone has one.
  • Sirens on a lip trill through the break, quietly, daily. The goal is a smooth blend ("mix"), not a louder chest voice.
  • Lower the volume as you approach the break, and don't let the throat squeeze.
  • Some singers use the flip on purpose: Jeff Buckley, Bon Iver, yodelling, the "cry" in country and in Indian film songs. Practise it as a controlled move with the register-flip drill.
Falsetto, head voice and "mix"
  • Falsetto: light, airy, thin folds. It's easy to get high in, but it's breathy.
  • Head voice: also light, but with a clearer, more connected tone.
  • Mix: a blend that carries some chest-voice strength higher up. It takes months of light, regular practice.
  • Start with quiet octave slides on "oo" or "ng". Never force the top.
I'm consistently flat (or sharp)
  • Flat: often low energy, tiredness, or a dark, swallowed vowel. Brighten the vowel slightly, give more breath support, keep the drone audible.
  • Sharp: usually tension or over-blowing. Sing softer, and relax the shoulders and jaw.
  • Check your tuning map: if one note of the scale is off (e.g. the 3rd is sharp), drill it with pitch matching.
My held notes wobble (or I can't do vibrato)
  • Steadiness comes from steady airflow. Do the breath hiss and long tones daily.
  • Real vibrato appears when the voice is free and supported. Don't fake it with the jaw. The vibrato drill alternates straight and vibrato tones so you learn to control it.
Fast, curly bits: riffs, runs, flips and ornaments

What sounds like "two notes at once" is almost always a very fast ornament:

  • Runs / riffs (melisma): several notes on one syllable, as in R&B, gospel and pop. Usually built from the pentatonic scale.
  • Grace notes / flips: a split-second touch of a neighbouring note before the main one.
  • Slides, scoops and fall-offs: gliding into or off a note (soul, rock, country).
  • Vibrato: a regular wobble on a held note, about 5–7 times a second.
  • In Indian music: kan (grace note), murki (quick 3–4 note turn), gamak (controlled oscillation between notes), meend (glide), andolan (slow swing).

Method: loop the line at 0.5–0.6×, turn on the guide tone, look at the shape of the curve, and copy it on "ah". Then drill the move on its own (runs, grace notes, slides, vibrato). Speed comes last. A fast run sung loosely sounds worse than a slow one sung exactly.

Microtones and "in-between" notes

Not all music sits on piano keys. Blues and rock bend the 3rd and 7th between minor and major ("blue notes"). Indian classical music places some notes differently, and sung against a drone a major 3rd tends to sit around 386¢ (piano: 400¢). For any song, follow the singer's curve: the reference line already contains their bends. In Settings, "Just intonation" shows drone-natural targets, and ±15¢ tolerance is there for fine work.

I run out of breath in long phrases
  • Plan breath points: listen for where the singer breathes, and mark them.
  • Inhale low and silently; don't start a long phrase with a loud, pushed onset.
  • Daily hiss exercise: aim for 25–30 s of even hiss.
My throat hurts

Stop for the day. Singing should never hurt. Common causes: singing too high or loud for too long, not warming up, dehydration, or pushing through a cold. Hoarseness or pain lasting more than two weeks, or a sudden loss of voice, needs an ENT doctor.

Indian music terms, explained

You don't need these for most songs. They're here for when you learn Indian film or classical songs, or switch Settings → Music style to Indian.

Sa, sargam, swara
Indian solfège. Sa Re Ga Ma Pa Dha Ni are the seven swaras (notes), like do-re-mi. Sa is the home note (tonic). It isn't a fixed pitch: each singer picks the Sa that suits their voice.
Komal / tivra
Flat / sharp versions of a note. Komal Re, Ga, Dha, Ni are a semitone lower (written lowercase: r g d n); tivra Ma is a semitone higher (M).
Saptak: mandra, madhya, taar
Octaves: low, middle and high. Written with a dot below (low) or above (high) the note.
Tanpura
A droning string instrument that plays Sa (and Pa) continuously, so you always hear the home note to tune against.
Raga, thaat
A raga is a melodic framework: which notes, how you move between them, and characteristic phrases. A thaat is a parent scale ragas are grouped under (Bilawal ≈ major scale).
Aroha / avaroha
The ascending / descending form of a scale or raga.
Alankar
Practice patterns over a scale (e.g. S R G, R G M, …), like scale sequences in Western training.
Meend
A smooth slide from one note to another, through everything in between.
Kan swar
A grace note: a split-second touch of another note before the main one.
Gamak (gamaka)
A controlled oscillation or shake between notes. In Carnatic music, gamakas are central to each raga's identity.
Murki
A quick, delicate turn of 3–4 notes, like a mini-run.
Andolan
A slow, gentle swing on a note.
Taan
A fast run of notes, the Indian cousin of a vocal riff.
Shruti
Fine pitch positions (microtones) within the octave. Also used to mean the chosen tonic pitch.
Riyaz
Daily practice.
Kattai / kali–safed
Ways of naming which key your Sa is in (Carnatic pitch-pipe numbers; Hindustani harmonium black/white keys).

About this app

Everything runs in your browser. Nothing you sing or record is uploaded. To learn a song, Swara Lab first separates the voice from the instruments with the Kim_Vocal_2 MDX-Net model (MIT licence; Spleeter as a faster fallback), running on your own computer in ONNX Runtime Web. It then traces the melody of the isolated voice with harmonic-summation salience and Viterbi tracking, in the spirit of Salamon & Gómez's Melodia. Your own singing is tracked live with the YIN pitch detector. Measured on 50 real songs against the true isolated vocal, this raised correctly traced notes from 55% to 87% and cut false melody (instruments mistaken for the singer) from 83% to 3% of non-singing moments. It isn't perfect: if a stretch looks wrong, use "Fix the melody guide" under the song. Chrome or Edge on a computer is recommended (needed for capturing YouTube audio).
